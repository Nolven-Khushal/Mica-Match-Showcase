# The Mica Match journey

How a "can you find this shade?" question from the sales floor became a photo-matching web app, an offline phone app and a catalogue of more than two thousand shades. Everything below is what actually happened, including the parts that did not work.

## 1. The brief

A laminate manufacturer sells through reps who visit shops. Again and again a shop owner holds up a sample, sometimes a rival's, and asks: *"Do you have something like this?"* The catalogue runs to dozens of PDFs and thousands of shades, so the rep could not answer on the spot.

The ask: **photograph the sample, get the closest shades from our own catalogue, with design number and name, in the shop, on a phone.** Three constraints came with it:

- Free. No paid APIs, no budget for servers.
- Phone first, used by reps with no training.
- The design number has to be exactly right, because it is what gets quoted and ordered.

## 2. Getting the catalogue into a database

The shades only existed as PDF pages: flattened raster images with no text layer, a swatch photo with a caption like `3094 RE 17 | AFRICAN CHERRY LIGHT` under it, and a different grid layout on almost every page.

**Attempt 1 – classic computer vision plus OCR.** Detect swatch blobs, OCR the caption, pair them by position. On 11 real pages it found about **40%** of the swatches, and the hit rate changed from page to page. Digit confusion was worse: `3183` read as `3103`. For a tool whose whole job is the design number, that is a business risk, not a cosmetic one. It was abandoned.

**Attempt 2 – let a vision-language model read each page.** Pages were rendered at two resolutions (one to read, one to crop) and read in parallel batches into structured JSON: design code, finish code, name, bounding box. On a page the CV pipeline got wrong, this got 10 out of 10, including telling `3075 California Ash` from `3076 White Ash`.

**Cross-checking instead of trusting.** The company's ERP product master gave an independent source of truth for design numbers. Every extracted code was checked against it. First batch: 77 of 77. Over all 8 catalogues (**1,596 swatches**) **86.2%** matched exactly. The rest were flagged, not dropped, and most turned out to be genuine drift between printed catalogue and ERP (a printed `LX3022` that is plain `3022` in the system), not extraction errors.

A decision that paid off later: **thickness is curated per catalogue by hand**. Joining on design number to the ERP looked like a free shortcut, but the ERP reuses design numbers across unrelated lines, so one 0.8 mm catalogue "joined" to 1 mm for a quarter of its rows. Testing the shortcut before building on it saved a wrong filter in the browse screen.

## 3. The matching engine: five models and three rejections

The first engine was a colour histogram plus texture descriptor. It passed a 16-swatch smoke test and **collapsed at real scale**: a mildly distorted query dropped the true match to rank 65 of 1,596.

Every candidate was then judged on the same harsh benchmark: 15 swatches distorted with rotation, exposure shift, colour cast, blur and downsampling.

| Candidate | Result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Colour + texture histogram | worst | collapsed at scale |
| CLIP | 13% top-1 | wrong invariance: it is trained on concepts, not fine grain |
| MobileNetV3 + colour blend | 27% top-1, 60% top-5 | first shipped engine |
| **DINOv2 (small) alone** | **33% top-1, 73% top-5** | beat the whole ensemble |
| DINOv2 + any colour blend | worse, monotonically | colour blending removed |
| A material-recognition network trained on synthetic renders | 20% top-1, 6x slower | rejected: it recognises *categories* (wood vs metal), not two similar oaks |

**The head.** Frozen DINOv2 embeddings were fed into a small trained projection head (two layers, metric learning). Training data was generated by simulating bad phone photos: 8 augmented views per swatch plus the clean one, 14,364 embeddings. Because the backbone was frozen and the embeddings cached, training took **41 seconds**. On a *harder* benchmark than before, top-1 went from 33% to **67%**, top-5 to 93%, top-10 to 100%.

**A product decision hidden in the numbers.** Even the best model is a *shortlist* finder. The UI shows the closest 15 and asks the rep to confirm with their eyes, the way reverse image search works, and never presents "the" answer. Presenting a single answer would only have made a good tool look unreliable.

## 4. Real photos disagree with the benchmark

A hand test on many real shades came out at roughly 70% right-first-try, close to the 67% benchmark, which was reassuring. Of the misses, 9 in 10 still had the correct shade in the list. The remaining few percent were absurd: a rich brown photographed as khaki matching a pale entry.

Root cause: a phone's auto white balance and exposure wash colour out far beyond what the training augmentation covered.

**The mistake.** The cheapest fix looked like correcting the *query photo* at inference time (grey-world white balance plus a saturation boost). On the benchmark, top-1 fell from **10/15 to 3/15**. The head had learned on uncorrected images, so correcting only one side moved the query out of the distribution it knew. The lesson became a rule: *a correction is either baked into training on both sides, or it is not applied at all.*

**The proper fix, done later**: a wider fade range plus a separate "veiling glare" augmentation, then retraining. After that, a colour re-rank in Lab space (hue counts, lightness half as much) lifted synthetic top-1 from 54 to 62 out of 80. Measured on 40 real tester photos, there was a systematic bias: real photos run warmer than catalogue swatches (median +3 on one colour axis, +8 on the other), so the bias is subtracted before ranking. The median rank of the right shade on those photos improved from 60 to about 28.

## 5. From laptop demo to a website

- **Phone app first.** React Native + Expo with a custom glass-style design system. It shipped with a live camera, three-shot capture (the three embeddings are averaged to cancel glare and white-balance drift), a "why this matched" breakdown and a share-with-customer button.
- **A reskin broke a feature.** Replacing the system camera with an in-app camera silently removed cropping, which had come free with the system one. The fix was a proper pan-and-pinch crop screen.
- **A bug that looked like a network problem.** After adding endpoints, the phone said "could not reach server". The running server had been started before the new routes existed, and a server without auto-reload never rereads its code. Rule written down: restart after any change.
- **The IP problem.** The server lived on a laptop and the phone needed its LAN address, which changed with every WiFi network. It came up several times a week.
- **Hosting without a card or a budget.** A free cloud VM failed at sign-up. A free tunnel hit its monthly data cap because every swatch was about 850 KB; serving 480 px thumbnails of roughly 13 KB made the allowance last about 30 times longer. The settled setup was a plain-HTML site served by the same API, reached through a free funnel, behind a shared access code with rate-limited login.
- **A learning loop.** Every match can be marked right or wrong; the photo, the shortlist and the true shade are stored. A retrain script holds out 25% of the real photos, promotes the new head only if it beats the old one, and keeps the previous head for clean testing. (Lesson: evaluating on photos the head trained on is meaningless.)

## 6. A feature built and killed

A stretch goal was "type a competitor's design number and get our nearest shade". A pilot was built end to end for one rival brand: 569 swatches extracted, scored against the catalogue, visually reviewed, wired into the API and the app.

It was removed the same day. Reviewing each row by eye cost an enormous number of model tokens (hundreds of thousands per batch of 35 rows) and the rate limit was hit repeatedly, with six brands and thousands of rows still to go. And the camera path already covered most real cases. The write-up kept the lessons: scope any revival to very-high-confidence matches only, and give the uncertain ones to a person who knows the catalogue by heart.

## 7. Catalogue completeness

The business owner set a rule: **nothing goes to a store until every shade of every catalogue is in the app.** A re-read showed the earlier database was a de-duplicated subset: one catalogue that looked like 55 shades really had more than 440, another showed 28 instead of about 300.

- New vector PDFs allowed clean extraction: each swatch is its own embedded picture, so crops arrive with no baked-in border or caption.
- Several extractors were needed (embedded pictures plus caption OCR, captions inside the frame, flattened pages, text layers) because no two supplier templates agreed.
- The data model changed to **catalogue membership**: a design is stored once, remembers every catalogue it appears in, but keeps every printed picture's fingerprint because different prints of one shade help matching.
- Along the way, three data-quality bugs in the source catalogues were documented rather than hidden: placeholder boxes with literal "170 w x 170 h" text, wrong crops on composite pages, and duplicate codes across pages.
- An honest negative result: swapping 90 old crops for clean ones changed accuracy by nothing. Coverage and real photos mattered more than crop quality.

## 8. Matching on the phone, with no server

With no free always-on server, the model moved onto the device.

- DINOv2 runs in the browser via ONNX Runtime (WebAssembly), the projection head and colour re-rank in JavaScript, and the shade index ships as static files.
- Verified against the server on 40 real photos: identical hit counts, **98% overlap** of the top 15, about **0.5 seconds** per photo on a laptop. Quantising the model to 22 MB cost accuracy (91.7% overlap), so the full-precision model shipped.
- The pack is bundled into an Android shell (a WebView plus a local static server). The first build crashed on an import I had pointed at the wrong package; the second ran on a real phone.
- Corrections made on the phone are stored as fingerprints only, never photos, and shared from settings as a small file.

## 9. The interface

A first round of four UI concepts, a hybrid chosen from them, then a final library design: a perspective-tunnel entrance (once a day), a stacked carousel of catalogue covers, a pull-out table stage with a fan of shades, a rotary dial menu, and a scan flow that starts at the camera. The brief to myself: one lighting model across the whole screen, light and dark themes, and no redesign after approval.

## 10. Store release and where it stands

A new personal developer account needs a closed test with at least 12 opted-in testers for 14 days before it may apply for production. That clock cannot be rushed, so it is running while the work above continues.

- Working: photo matching (server and fully offline), browse by catalogue, thickness and design number, search, colour finder, boards, history, enquiry by message, share, team page.
- Open: iOS (the web version already runs on iPhone; a store build is not made), a free always-on server, and a retrain once a few hundred real tester photos are in.

## What I would tell myself at the start

1. Build the benchmark before the model, and keep it harder than the real thing.
2. Test a "free" shortcut on real data before building on it.
3. Never patch one side of a trained pipeline.
4. Treat a ground-truth source as a cross-check, not a replacement; flag, do not drop.
5. Kill a feature when its cost per unit of value is wrong, and write down why.
